From conversations to structured evidence for founders

FoundersBrew's AI agent helps early-stage founders move beyond validating their assumptions to gathering evidence from their target audience through agentic discovery interviews.

DomainAI-Enabled SaaS / Founder Research
Product areaAdaptive User Interviews
ScopeHuman Agent interaction, Founder onboarding, Hypotheses Validation

The problem

The journey from idea to MVP depends on gathering the right evidence to make informed product decisions. However, founders often enter discovery with assumptions about their ideas and unintentionally seek validation rather than genuine insights. Leading questions encourage responses in a particular direction, while feedback scattered across calls, messages, documents, and spreadsheets makes it difficult to identify meaningful patterns.

As a result, founders risk mistaking encouragement for evidence and making build decisions based on weak signals, potentially leading them to build the wrong product.

The challenge is the lack of a structured system that guides founders through discovery with their target audience and helps them gather reliable evidence.

The solution

An interview agent that adapts to the conversation and a system that synthesizes the evidence.

Founder context

Founders answer a few questions about their idea, target audience and the problem they are trying to solve. The system combines their inputs with secondary research to build context for discovery.

Adaptive interview

The AI agent uses this context to conduct interviews, asking follow-up questions, listening, probing and clarifying based on what participants share.

Evidence

The system tells which hypotheses got validated and what was challenged.

Outcome

Established foundations for trustworthy AI

Every finding can be traced back to the transcript, biased exchanges are caught before they reach the synthesis, and new checks can be added as the product grows.

Founders stayed in control, without hiding inconvenient evidence

Founders could exclude interviews, but only by giving a reason, and the decision stayed visible and reversible. That protected the research from the confirmation bias the product exists to prevent.

Bias detection went from a vague idea to buildable rules

The bias types, edge cases and validation rules gave engineering clear, testable requirements to build from.

Understanding the landscape

The first thing I did was to understand where the FoundersBrew interview agent would stand. I went onto understand who our competitors were, what other adaptive interview agents were available.

This not only provided direction but also helped me think how this agentic system should work

But will people willingly talk to an AI agent?

When the idea of an AI interviewer first came up, my immediate question was whether people would actually feel comfortable having a conversation with one. AI-led interviewing wasn't entirely new. Competitors were already developing compelling conversational research products, but I wanted to understand whether there was evidence of genuine participant willingness, beyond the technology itself.

Research by Wuttke et al. (2025) demonstrated the feasibility of LLM-led interviews, while Barari et al. (2026) found that AI-assisted conversations could elicit more detailed and informative responses.

More importantly, Chopra and Haaland (2026) found that 90% of respondents expressed interest in an AI-led interview, compared with 89% for a traditional survey. In their separate AI-interview studies, over 90% of participants who started completed the interview, even though completing it wasn't required for payment.

This was reinforced by Gheyi et al. (2026), who analysed 66 completed AI-led interviews and found that 90.9% felt comfortable answering questions, while 89.4% said they would participate again.

There was also evidence supporting the importance of relevance. Groves, Presser and Dipko examined how interest in a research topic influences people's decisions to participate, supporting the rationale for approaching respondents with a subject that matters to them.

Taken together, these findings gave us a credible foundation. AI could conduct meaningful interviews, participants showed willingness to engage, and topic relevance offered a reason to take part. Combined with competitors already demonstrating the product concept, the FoundersBrew team chose a me-too approach, bringing AI-led interviewing into the early-stage founder discovery workflow.

Visual design direction

As FoundersBrew prepared for its accelerator applications in early 2027, I prioritised a lightweight, scalable UI that aligned with the product's early-stage goals and engineering constraints.

I used reusable components, consistent interaction patterns and progressive disclosure to create an intuitive experience while reducing implementation complexity and maintenance effort.

This was a deliberate design decision that allowed the team to iterate faster and focus engineering resources on validating the product's core strength which is delivering high-quality adaptive AI interviews.

Human in the loop

I designed the workflow around a human-in-the-loop approach, where AI handles research and analysis while founders retain control over key decisions. The challenge was deciding where the system should take responsibility and where founders needed to remain involved.

This shaped three parts of the experience, how founders provide context, choose what gets tested, and control the evidence used in synthesis.

Helping founders provide the right context

The process begins with founders describing their ideas, target audiences, and the problems they want to solve. FoundersBrew's questionnaire is not a conventional onboarding form. It is the input layer for a research system. The four answers influence which market the system investigates, which users and conversations it looks for, which competitors and substitutes it identifies, which hypotheses it generates, and the context supplied to the discovery agent.

That makes the objective different from simply maximizing form completion. The questionnaire should optimize for answers that are specific, researchable, honest about uncertainty, and recognizably the founder's own thinking.

Reduce the effort required to understand each question, while preserving the effort required to think about the answer.

Why I added 'not sure yet' as a valid response

Reduced cognitive load

I gave founders a way to move forward without having to formulate an answer they weren't ready to provide.

Prevented forced assumptions

I reduced the risk of founders inventing information simply to complete the questionnaire.

Preserved research integrity

I ensured the system could distinguish between assumptions founders had actually expressed and areas where they remained uncertain.

Why I decided against integrating AI in the onboarding questionnaire?

One thing very clear about FoundersBrew is that FoundersBrew should use AI to investigate the founder's thinking, not necessarily to generate that thinking for them. At this point Founders need to articulate their own assumptions before those assumptions are challenged. It's also the founder's first moment of structured reflection.

More friction

A simple questionnaire becomes an unpredictable conversation with additional questions before the founder gets any value.

False precision

AI might push founders to elaborate on assumptions they haven't verified, making guesses sound more concrete than they really are.

Unnecessary complexity

This would add cost, latency, and another experience to design and test without knowing whether it improves the research output.

Letting founders decide what gets tested first

Once founders provide their context, FoundersBrew combines their inputs with secondary research to generate hypotheses, ranked by risk. While the system recommends which assumptions to test first, the final decision remains with the founder.

Initially, this felt like a straightforward way to give founders control. But as I considered how the adaptive agent would actually conduct interviews, I noticed a mismatch between the interaction and the system's behaviour.

Founders didn't necessarily need to choose which hypotheses were included or excluded. They needed to influence where the agent should begin. So I shifted from a selection model to a prioritisation model.

Giving founders control over the evidence

Not all interviews are of the same quality, and the founder can decide if they want to remove a particular interview from the synthesis, if they spot a quality issue, or a target audience mismatch. So I designed an option for founders to exclude questionable interviews without deleting the original research.

The interface explains how this decision impacts hypothesis statuses and other findings, while allowing interviews to be restored later. This ensures founders can intervene when evidence quality is in question rather than relying entirely on AI-generated conclusions.

But the human control can introduce another form of bias...

While exploring human-in-the-loop patterns in AI-first products, I realised that giving users more control doesn't automatically make a system more trustworthy. Allowing founders to exclude interviews introduced an unintended risk.

What if founders removed interviews simply because the findings challenged their assumptions?

This could reinforce the very confirmation bias FoundersBrew was designed to address. I needed to consider the difference between giving founders control over research quality and allowing them to selectively remove inconvenient evidence.

...so how might we prevent founders from selectively excluding interviews that challenge their assumptions, while still giving them control over the quality of their research evidence?

This pushed me to look beyond conventional UX patterns and led me to behavioural design. I came across research on accountability and decision-making, particularly how asking people to justify their choices can encourage more deliberate thinking. It made me reconsider something we often prioritise in UX, 'reducing friction'

What if removing friction wasn't always the right thing to do?

In this case, making interview exclusion effortless could allow founders to dismiss uncomfortable evidence without reflecting on why they were doing so. I explored introducing a small amount of intentional friction at the point of exclusion.

Founders would be asked to provide a reason for excluding an interview and understand how that decision could affect their findings. The original research would remain accessible, and exclusions could be reversed.

The intention was to create a moment where they could reconsider whether they were questioning the quality of the evidence or simply disagreeing with what it revealed.

Making exclusion decisions visible

Instead of simply marking the interview as excluded, I designed the interface to keep the reason and explanation visible alongside the decision, while providing an option to restore it. The behavioural reasoning was to make founders aware of their past decisions and encourage them to reconsider those choices when reviewing their research.

The outcome

Rather than adding human oversight as a final approval step, I embedded it throughout the research workflow. Founders provide the context, choose what to investigate, and decide which evidence to trust. The AI supports them through research, prioritization, interviews, and synthesis.

This creates a workflow where AI does the analytical work, while founders remain responsible for the decisions that shape their ideas.

The agent workflow and bias detection

To ensure that founders weren't making decisions based on misleading evidence it was important to get it the bias detection right, but where do you even begin when bias can take so many forms?

Starting with uncertainty

The first thing I needed to understand was which biases we could realistically detect. Trying to address every type of bias in the first version wasn't feasible. Bias detection would need to be introduced incrementally, with the system evolving and becoming more capable over time.

After conducting secondary research, I narrowed the scope to four types of biases that the first version would focus on

The design decision: I prioritised the biases most directly connected to agent behaviour and insight reliability, rather than attempting comprehensive bias detection in the first release. The selection was guided by three factors: impact on research quality, detectability from interview transcripts, and implementation feasibility.

Bridging UX research and engineering

One challenge I encountered while designing the AI interviewer was that bias isn't always obvious from a single question. The same wording could be leading in one conversation and a legitimate follow-up in another.

I needed to communicate this nuance to engineering in a way that could inform the bias-detection framework. I translated these considerations into five test scenarios.

I defined what the system should consider before flagging an exchange as biased

  • Who introduced the idea? Was it the participant or the agent?
  • Was an assumption introduced as a fact?
  • Did the question suggest a preferred answer?
  • Did the participant's subsequent response appear influenced by that framing?
  • Is there an equally plausible, non-biased interpretation?

Key requirement: A question shouldn't be marked as biased solely because of its wording. The surrounding conversation matters.

Translating research requirements into validation rules

Defining what constitutes bias was only part of the challenge. I also needed to consider how the system could produce findings that founders could trust and verify. Working with engineering, I helped separate bias detection into two responsibilities.

Contextual evaluation

The AI examines the surrounding conversation to determine whether an exchange may be biased. This includes understanding what the participant said previously, who introduced an idea and whether the agent's question could reasonably be interpreted as neutral.

Deterministic validation

Once the AI identifies potential bias, a separate set of rules checks whether the finding is correctly structured and supported by evidence from the transcript.

I collaborated with engineering to define six validation requirements and the expected behaviour when a check failed.

RuleDeterministic checkIf it fails
01. Category validationIs the finding one of the four defined bias types?Reject
02. Reference validationDoes the finding reference a real question asked by the agent?Reject
03. Evidence validationDoes the quoted evidence match the transcript word for word?Request correction
04. Format validationAre all required details present in the correct format?Request correction
05. Assessment validationIs the finding explicitly assessed as bias rather than an uncertain interpretation?Reject
06. Duplicate validationHas the same issue already been reported for the same question?Merge or remove duplicate

My contribution was defining the research-quality criteria, test scenarios and expected validation behaviours, while engineering was responsible for implementing the detection and validation logic.

How this shows up in the product and affects synthesis

I used my experience in UX research and did a desk research to define how to handle potentially compromised interview data. One rule for any research finding is that it should remain grounded in what participants independently said or did, rather than conclusions shaped by the interviewer's assumptions.

This influenced an important decision for FoundersBrew

Exchanges flagged for bias would be excluded from evidence synthesis, but remain visible to the founder, making it clear for the founder to understand why certain responses were not considered.

The interview experience

When I was designing the interview experience, I first wrote down how the experience feels like when I conduct a one-to-one user interview, and prioritised what should go in phase one and what can wait, based on what people expect from an agent.

I developed the participant journey by referring findings from research on AI-moderated interviews & conversational interaction and mapped the HAX guidelines to it.

I with the engineering team created a fixed question prototype on ElevenLabs to test the interaction with the users and get their feedback. The ElevenLabs prototype had a fallback textbox, that was later incorporated in the design, because I observed users using the textbox to type in their answers when they were uncomfortable or it was inconvenient to speak.

My takeaways

Clarity comes through action.

When faced with uncertainty, breaking a complex problem into smaller, testable decisions helped me move forward rather than waiting for complete clarity.

Intentional friction can be good friction.

Not every interaction needs to be effortless. Sometimes encouraging people to pause, question an outcome and take responsibility for their decisions creates a more meaningful experience.

Combining LLMs with deterministic workflows creates a more accountable system.

LLMs can interpret conversational context, while deterministic checks make outputs more structured, traceable and easier to verify.

Trust comes from transparency, not certainty.

AI-generated findings shouldn't be trusted simply because they sound convincing. Making the underlying evidence visible and allowing people to question an assessment is just as important as producing the assessment itself.
