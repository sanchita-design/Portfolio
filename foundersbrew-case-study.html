How I designed FoundersBrew’s AI interview agent to accelerate discovery, so the first user conversation happens in hours, not weeks

FoundersBrew's AI agent helps early-stage founders move beyond validating their assumptions to gathering evidence from their target audience through agentic discovery interviews.

DomainAI-Enabled SaaS / Founder Research
Product areaAdaptive User Interviews
ScopeHuman Agent interaction, Founder onboarding, Hypotheses Validation

The problem

Founders often enter discovery with assumptions about their ideas and unintentionally seek validation rather than genuine insights. Feedback is often scattered across calls, messages, documents, and spreadsheets, which makes it difficult to identify meaningful patterns. As a result, founders risk mistaking encouragement for evidence and making build decisions based on weak signals, potentially leading them to build the wrong product.

The challenge is the lack of a structured system that guides founders through discovery with their target audience and helps them gather reliable evidence.

The solution

An interview agent that adapts to the conversation and a system that synthesizes the evidence.

Founder context

Founders answer a few questions about their idea, target audience and the problem they are trying to solve. The system combines their inputs with secondary research to build context for discovery.

Adaptive interview

The AI agent uses this context to conduct interviews, asking follow-up questions, listening, probing and clarifying based on what participants share.

Evidence

The system tells which hypotheses got validated and what was challenged.

Outcome

~68% interview completion rates

Observed in the 38 interviews conducted by 7 founders who are FoundersBrew early product collaborators

85% first time success

Founders were able set up and share interview link successfully on their first attempt, without any help.

Visual design direction

As FoundersBrew prepared for its accelerator applications in early 2027, I prioritised a lightweight, scalable UI that aligned with the product's early-stage goals and engineering constraints.

I used reusable components, consistent interaction patterns and progressive disclosure to create an intuitive experience while reducing implementation complexity and maintenance effort.

This was a deliberate design decision that allowed the team to iterate faster and focus engineering resources on validating the product's core strength which is delivering high-quality adaptive AI interviews.

The founder context and the challenge

Before using their interview credits, founders need to review hypotheses generated by AI. I wanted to make this process straightforward, giving them a clear way to examine the AI's suggestions and assess the reasoning behind them.....because what if the AI made mistakes or was wrong?

Designing a journey focused on transparency and control

The setup journey takes founders through six screens, beginning with a review of how their idea was understood and ending with a shareable interview link. Each screen introduces a specific opportunity to check the AI's work or make a decision.

How FoundersBrew AI + deterministic workflow understood the founder’s idea

The journey begins by showing founders how the system interpreted their idea.

Founders see whether the system has understood their idea correctly, and also get a way to refine or edit it.

A trade-off: Each run adds costs for FoundersBrew, so a hard limit of 2 runs has to be set.

What the system suggested that the founder should test with the users

These suggestions came from a workflow that used AI models to do secondary research and generate hypotheses, to make sure that each hypothesis has an origin. It also meant that the AI could have missed a few things or added a few irrelevant hypotheses. In any case, the founder should be in full control to correct those mistakes.

Where the idea stood today and the entry to the interview setup process

Prioritise

Founders decide which hypotheses matter most by arranging them in their preferred order. The top three then become the focus of the interview.

Review and create

Before the idea is locked, founders see a final review of what the interview will test and what it will use, allowing them to check the details before creating it.

Link ready

Once the interview has been created, founders receive a link that is ready to share.

Design principles to enforce trust and control

Honest about what it is

Every hypothesis carries an Inferred label, making it clear that the assumption has yet to be proven through responses from real people.

Traceable to evidence

Founders can open each hypothesis to see where it came from, with a supporting line that explains its source.

The founder stays in control

Throughout the process, founders can update their idea, flag anything that does not fit, add what is missing, and decide the order of their hypotheses.

The interview experience

When I was designing the interview experience, I first wrote down how the experience feels like when I conduct a one-to-one user interview, and prioritised what should go in phase one and what can wait, based on what people expect from an agent.

I developed the participant journey by referring findings from research on AI-moderated interviews & conversational interaction and mapped the HAX guidelines to it.

Testing with a prototype

I with the engineering team created a fixed question prototype on ElevenLabs to test the interaction with the users and get their feedback. The ElevenLabs prototype had a fallback textbox, that was later incorporated in the design, because I observed users using the textbox to type in their answers when they were uncomfortable or it was inconvenient to speak.

What changed after testing the prototype?

I also found different questions require different rhythms

Based on the testing the prototype with users and combining it with qualitative research methods from The Mom Test and Contextual Inquiry, focusing on how different questions affect memory retrieval and speech patterns I defined the agent’s listening patience, response timing, and follow-up behavior.

ArchetypeExample questionTurn-taking behaviorFollow-up strategy
1. Screening / Habitual“Who usually cooks meals at home?”600–800ms. Respond quickly once a complete fact has been established.Branch or confirm baseline context.
2. Episodic Recall“Tell me about the last time you danced at home.”1.8–2.5s. Allow longer pauses and hold through fillers such as “um” or unfinished phrases like “and then…”Explore the sequence of events, tools, or environment.
3. Workflow / Process“Walk me through raising your last invoice.”1.5–2.0s. Maintain the listening state through pauses between steps in a process.Investigate the messiest or slowest step.
4. Affective / Stakes“How did that experience feel?”1.5–2.2s. Allow space for reflection, using a soft acknowledgment such as “mm-hmm” only during longer stories exceeding 12 seconds.Briefly reflect the emotion expressed, then explore how frequently it occurs.

Designing for how people actually talk with trade-offs

Real participants pause mid-thought, answer questions ahead of time, give vague replies, and lose track of long prompts. I translated these behaviours into clear rules that engineering could implement and test.

People pause mid-thought

A fixed 800ms silence threshold risked cutting off longer stories, so I introduced context aware pause detection based on each question type.

Trade-off: Slightly slower responses in exchange for fewer interruptions and more complete stories.

People answer ahead

Participants sometimes answer multiple questions at once. For example, “I cook, and actually last night our stove broke…” already provides habitual context and a friction point. The agent marks the Episodic and Friction slots as captured and moves directly to an Affective follow-up.

Trade-off: Less rigid question sequencing in exchange for fewer repetitive questions and a more natural conversation.

People give low-effort answers

Repeatedly probing vague responses like “It was fine” can make conversations uncomfortable without adding meaningful insights. I capped follow-ups at two, with the agent acknowledging the response and moving on after two consecutive low-effort answers.

Trade-off: Less depth per topic in exchange for fewer mid-call hang-ups.

People struggle with multi-part prompts

Questions like “Share your last invoice experience, what do you remember, how was it?” require participants to recall and evaluate an experience simultaneously. I introduced a single-question-per-turn rule, separating memory recall from emotional evaluation.

Trade-off: More conversational turns in exchange for clearer questions and easier turn-taking.

People struggle to hold context

A full scrolling transcript divided attention and made the conversation feel scripted. I kept only the active question visible in high-contrast text, reducing memory load and preserving the voice-first experience.

Trade-off: Less visible conversation history in exchange for lower memory load and a voice-first experience.

My takeaways

Clarity comes through action.

When faced with uncertainty, breaking a complex problem into smaller, testable decisions helped me move forward rather than waiting for complete clarity.

Intentional friction can be good friction.

Not every interaction needs to be effortless. Sometimes encouraging people to pause, question an outcome and take responsibility for their decisions creates a more meaningful experience.

Combining LLMs with deterministic workflows creates a more accountable system.

LLMs can interpret conversational context, while deterministic checks make outputs more structured, traceable and easier to verify.

Trust comes from transparency, not certainty.

AI-generated findings shouldn't be trusted simply because they sound convincing. Making the underlying evidence visible and allowing people to question an assessment is just as important as producing the assessment itself.
