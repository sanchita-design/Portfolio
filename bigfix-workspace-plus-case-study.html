Redesigning BigFix's self service application laid the foundation for the next gen of the product, while also resulting in ~ 45% reduction in task completion time for employees

Turning a technical endpoint-management tool into an employee-facing workspace.

HCL BigFix helps organizations manage and secure employee devices at scale. Its Self Service Assistant, or SSA, gave employees a way to access software, complete device actions, and respond to compliance requirements.

But as BigFix expanded beyond endpoint protection, SSA had to evolve too.

The long-term vision was to make it part of Workspace+ — an employee-facing platform where people could access software, manage device needs, and get IT support without needing to understand the systems behind it.

My role was to improve the existing SSA experience while helping establish the foundations for that broader product direction.

The problem

Employees were using a system designed around IT infrastructure, not around them.

The existing SSA was primarily understood as a software catalogue and compliance tool.

It supported application downloads and IT-driven actions, but the experience had grown around backend systems rather than employee needs.

As a result, even relatively simple tasks required people to understand technical labels, categories, and interaction patterns before they could get something done.

Our early review of the product and user interactions exposed recurring issues around:

  • Unclear information hierarchy
  • Jargon-heavy language
  • Scattered actions
  • Inconsistent interaction patterns
  • Limited support for scanning and discovery
  • Workflows that reflected internal system logic rather than employee intent

The issue wasn’t a lack of functionality.

The product was asking employees to understand the system before the system could help them.

This was the gap we needed to close.

The solution

One employee experience for finding software, taking action and getting help.

Instead of treating SSA as a catalogue, we began thinking about it as the front door to an employee’s IT experience.

That meant designing for two connected situations.

When employees know what they need

Make it faster to discover software, understand available actions, and complete routine tasks.

When employees don’t know what they need

Help them describe what is happening and guide them toward a useful resolution without requiring them to understand internal categories or ticketing systems first.

Together, those ideas became the foundation for the broader Workspace+ experience.

I know what I need
Search → Browse → Understand → Act
I need help
Describe issue → Understand context → Recommend path → Resolve or raise request

Both paths converge into Workspace+.

What changed

~45%
Reduction in task completion time

In usability sessions, new users took roughly 14–16 minutes on average to open a ticket before the redesign. After the redesign, that dropped to approximately 6–8 minutes. Based on task-based usability sessions with 9 participants.

10–15%
Improvement in ticket classification

The redesigned request flow helped classify tickets more accurately, contributing to faster triage and resolution during alpha and beta evaluation.

Foundations for the next-generation experience

How did we get there?

The work started with a practical question:

How could we improve the experience employees had today without designing ourselves into a corner for the product we wanted tomorrow?

That meant working on two tracks at once: improving the immediate experience and defining the interaction foundations that could scale into Workspace+.

01 — Understanding the system before redesigning the interface

The interface was only one layer of the problem.

Before changing screens, I needed to understand where SSA sat within the broader BigFix ecosystem.

I worked with stakeholders to understand how the product was perceived internally, what it currently enabled employees to do, and what Workspace+ was intended to become.

The gap was significant.

SSA today

  • A basic catalogue for software downloads
  • A compliance-oriented utility
  • A backend-driven admin experience
  • A product with no clear employee-facing position

The intended future

  • A core employee interface
  • A self-service hub with clearer language and guided actions
  • A system that could support a wider set of employee needs
  • An experience that could sit alongside products such as Jira, Zendesk, and ServiceNow while making use of BigFix’s device-management strengths

This gave us a long-term direction without forcing us to solve the entire product at once.

SSA today

  • Technical
  • Backend-driven
  • Catalogue-oriented
  • Fragmented actions
  • Employee adapts to system

Workspace+

  • Employee-facing
  • Guided
  • Task-oriented
  • Connected journeys
  • System adapts to employee

Start small, but design toward the larger system

The first step was not to redesign everything.

We deliberately focused on improving a high-frequency part of the existing product while using that work to establish reusable principles for the future experience.

The plan was:

01

Develop context

Understand the current product and the wider employee ecosystem.

02

Start small

Identify the smallest useful problem we could solve well.

03

Set foundations

Establish interaction patterns that could support more complex future workflows.

04

Expand

Use those foundations to explore smarter, more contextual employee experiences.

That allowed the project to balance short-term product needs with the longer-term Workspace+ direction.

Understand → Simplify → Establish → Expand
Existing SSA → Improved self-service → Support workflows → Workspace+

02 — Understanding where employees were struggling

The product had functionality. What it lacked was clarity.

I began with existing SSA users who regularly used the application for software and common employee tasks.

The goal was to understand:

  • What they used SSA for
  • How they expected common tasks to work
  • Where they became confused
  • How they searched for things
  • How much of the product’s language made sense to them

Several recurring patterns appeared.

Users struggled to interpret information distributed across the interface.

Similar actions behaved differently depending on where they appeared.

Some essential actions were difficult to discover.

And much of the interface assumed employees were already familiar with technical terminology and internal system structure.

That turned simple actions into unnecessary cognitive work.

“I know what I want to do. I just don’t always know where the product expects me to go.”

Information scattered

Important information lives across multiple areas.

Actions hard to predict

Similar actions appear and behave differently.

Efficiency gaps

Frequent actions require unnecessary steps.

Visual hierarchy

Users struggle to understand what deserves attention first.

Reframing the problem

The research changed the focus of the redesign.

The question was no longer:

How do we modernize the SSA interface?

It became:

How might we help employees understand what they can do, find it quickly, and act without needing to understand the underlying system?

That led to four design priorities:

  • Reduce cognitive overhead
  • Improve visual hierarchy
  • Simplify navigation
  • Make actions easier to understand and predict

03 — Simplifying the everyday self-service experience

Before adding more capability, we needed to remove friction from what already existed.

The first redesign focused on the core SSA experience.

A number of interface problems initially looked unrelated, but they could be traced back to one larger issue:

the product exposed its internal structure more clearly than the employee’s task.

So rather than solving each screen independently, I organized the redesign around a small set of interaction principles.

Put the action where the decision happens

In the existing experience, actions could be separated from the content they affected.

That forced employees to first interpret an item, then search elsewhere for what they could do with it.

I changed the interaction model so primary actions sat next to the relevant object. The employee could understand:

  • What is this?
  • What state is it in?
  • What can I do next?

without changing context.

Before

  • Object here
  • Action elsewhere

After

  • Object + status + primary action together

Actions belong to the thing they affect.

Reduce the number of interaction models

The product used multiple ways of presenting similar information.

For example, employees could encounter grid-based catalogues, list views, action requests, and other variations depending on where they were in the product. That increased learning cost.

I explored consolidating these into a smaller number of predictable patterns so employees did not need to relearn the interface from one task to another.

A unified list-based pattern gave us a better balance between density, hierarchy, and action visibility.

Existing grid → Wireframe exploration → Final list
  • Better scanning
  • Status visible
  • Actions stay in context

Search should work the way employees think

The existing experience was heavily dependent on navigation and product categories.

But employees often begin with a word, task, or problem rather than knowledge of how the organization classifies it. Search therefore became a more important entry point.

I explored a global search model that could help employees find relevant content across SSA rather than forcing them to first choose the correct part of the system.

Employee thinks

“VPN”

System terminology

“Secure remote access”

The interface should bridge the two.

Categories should guide, not constrain

Categories were still useful for browsing, but they needed to behave as flexible navigation rather than rigid system architecture.

I reduced the visual weight of category icons, introduced clearer labeling, and treated categories as one way of getting to an item rather than the only way.

This made the catalogue easier to scan while preserving support for employees who preferred browsing over search.

Designing the micro-decisions

Much of the improvement came from small interaction decisions rather than one dramatic feature. For example:

  • Selected items remain visually recognizable
  • Relevant status is visible without opening an item
  • Time-sensitive information avoids unnecessary mental calculation
  • Recommended content is clearly differentiated
  • Selected categories persist when useful
  • Contextual help appears close to where employees need it

Individually, these choices are small.

Together, they reduce hesitation and make the interface easier to understand at a glance.

Then we found the next problem

Self-service works well when employees know what they need.

A better catalogue made routine tasks faster and easier.

But while exploring the larger Workspace+ opportunity, another problem became clear.

Employees do not always know what they need.

They often start with:

“Something isn’t working.”

Not:

“I need to open a ticket in category X, under service Y, with subtype Z.”

This changed the next phase of the work.

If Workspace+ was going to become a real employee-facing experience, it needed to help people even when they could not already classify their own problem.

04 — Designing around employee intent, not ticket structure

Support systems often start with classification. Employees start with a problem.

To understand how employees asked for help, we reviewed service-request patterns and mapped the types of requests employees raised.

The current process was largely organized around the organization’s internal systems.

Employees were often expected to select the right category, service, or ticket path before they had enough information to know what the issue actually was. That created a mismatch:

The system thinks in

  • Services
  • Categories
  • Queues
  • Ticket types

The employee thinks in

  • “My password stopped working.”
  • “My laptop is slow.”
  • “I need access.”
  • “I can’t open this application.”

Workspace+ translates one into the other.

The opportunity was to design the interface around the second model while still gathering the structure the organization needed behind the scenes.

The hypothesis

We explored a simple hypothesis:

If employees could describe their issue in plain language, the system could progressively gather context and guide them toward the right resolution.

That might mean:

  • Solving the problem without a ticket
  • Suggesting a relevant action
  • Showing supporting information
  • Or collecting the right context before a request was submitted

The experience would no longer begin with:

“Which category does this belong to?”

It would begin with:

“What are you trying to do?”

Defining the information architecture behind the experience

A conversational entry point still needs structure underneath it.

I mapped common employee questions, information requirements, and potential resolution paths to understand which pieces of context were required at different points in the journey.

The goal was not to expose this complexity.

The goal was to understand it well enough that the interface could hide most of it.

The resulting architecture helped connect employee language to:

  • Relevant information
  • Troubleshooting steps
  • Device context
  • Support actions
  • And ticket creation when necessary
“My device password isn’t working.” → Access issue → Device + employee context → Reset, check device, show troubleshooting → Still unresolved → create request

05 — Designing the request flow

We tested different ways of moving from a vague problem to a useful request.

Early wireframes explored how much guidance the product should provide and when.

The challenge was finding a balance.

Ask too little and the organization receives an incomplete request.

Ask too much and Workspace+ becomes another complicated ticket form.

Several iterations helped refine the model.

Version 1 — Start with categories

The earliest concept relied more heavily on predefined categories and common requests.

This gave the employee structure, but it still assumed that they understood how their problem mapped to the organization’s taxonomy.

That was too close to the existing model.

Version 2 — Start with the employee’s intent

The next iteration moved the employee’s own description earlier in the flow.

Instead of choosing from a system hierarchy first, they could begin by describing what they were trying to do or what had gone wrong. The system could then use that information to narrow the next step.

This felt closer to how people naturally ask for help.

Version 3 — Progressively gather only the context we still need

The final direction combined open input with structured guidance.

Employees could describe the problem, see relevant options or suggestions, and provide additional context only when it was necessary.

If the issue could not be resolved through self-service, that context could move forward into the support request. The system still got the information it needed.

The employee did not have to provide it all upfront.

V1 · Category first → V2 · Intent first → V3 · Intent + progressive context

Guidance should help, not trap the employee

As we iterated on the request flow, feedback exposed another important constraint:

employees would not always follow the ideal path.

Someone might:

  • Enter a vague or incomplete query
  • Ignore a suggestion
  • Select the wrong category
  • Know exactly what they need and want to move quickly
  • Fail to find a useful recommendation
  • Want to raise a ticket regardless

The design therefore needed escape routes.

The system could suggest and guide, but employees still needed a clear way to recover when the recommendation was wrong or incomplete.

That principle became important throughout the final experience.

“I don’t know the right category”

Allow the system to infer and let the employee correct it.

“None of these suggestions help”

Keep a clear path to create a request.

“I know exactly what I need”

Provide a fast path.

“My request could fit multiple categories”

Use context progressively rather than forcing an early decision.

06 — Bringing the experience together

The final design turns support into a guided conversation.

The final experience carried the same principles established in the catalogue redesign into support.

  • Start with familiar employee language.
  • Use information the system already has.
  • Ask for additional context progressively.
  • Recommend a resolution when one is available.
  • And make the transition to a human-supported request clear when it is not.

Find common requests quickly

The redesigned entry point surfaces frequently used actions and supports keyword search.

This gives experienced employees a fast path while still supporting people who need guidance.

Describe the issue in your own words

Employees can begin with natural language rather than navigating a ticket taxonomy.

The system can use the request to surface relevant options and gather the next piece of context.

The interaction is intentionally progressive so employees do not face a long form before they know whether a ticket is even necessary.

Gather context progressively instead of asking everything upfront.

Keep context attached to the request

If self-service is not enough, the information already collected should not disappear.

The request experience carries relevant details forward so the employee can review what will be submitted rather than starting over. This also gives support teams better context when the request reaches them.

Make success unambiguous

Submitting a request is not the end of the employee’s experience.

The confirmation state explains that the request was successfully created and gives the employee clarity about what happens next.

That feedback matters especially in enterprise systems, where uncertainty can lead employees to repeat actions or raise duplicate requests.

Clear confirmation reduces uncertainty after submission.

Designing for reality, not the perfect flow

The final design also accounted for the moments where the system could not confidently help.

  • Recommendations could be wrong.
  • Information could be incomplete.
  • A user could reject a suggestion.
  • A support request could still be urgent.

So instead of optimizing only the ideal journey, the interaction model preserved employee control throughout.

The system could guide the employee without becoming another gatekeeper.

What this work established

We weren’t just redesigning SSA.

The immediate work improved catalogue usability and support-request flows.

But more importantly, it established a different model for the product.

One where software, device actions, information, and support could begin to feel like parts of the same employee experience rather than separate IT systems.

That was the larger Workspace+ opportunity.

What I learned

Efficiency is more than speed

The reduction in task time did not come from one feature.

It came from many small design decisions working together: clearer navigation, stronger hierarchy, more predictable actions, reduced ambiguity, better feedback, and fewer unnecessary decisions.

Short-term fixes need a long-term model

The project required improving the experience employees had immediately while simultaneously designing toward Workspace+.

That meant resisting quick fixes that solved one screen but created another interaction pattern.

The more useful question became:

Can this decision solve today’s problem and still belong in the product we want to build tomorrow?

That became one of the most important principles I took from the project.
