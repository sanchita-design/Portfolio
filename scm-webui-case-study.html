Streamlining Security Compliance: The SCM WebUI Redesign

BigFix is a B2B enterprise endpoint management platform. Within it, Security Compliance Manager helps organisations define, deploy, monitor and maintain cybersecurity compliance across environments that can contain hundreds of thousands of endpoints.

I worked on the broader modernisation of Security Compliance Manager across several interconnected administrator workflows. This case study focuses on creating and configuring a custom checklist—one representative part of that larger transformation—to show how we approached complexity, legacy constraints and expert-user needs.

DomainCybersecurity / Enterprise Endpoint Security
Product areaSecurity Compliance Management
ScopeUX, UI, interaction design, workflow design

The problem

Security Compliance Manager supported a set of complex, interconnected cybersecurity workflows.

Administrators could create and deploy custom checklists, maintain and synchronise them as external standards changed, manage target-device lists, and identify non-compliant devices for remediation.

As the product evolved, these workflows accumulated significant complexity.

Administrators had to navigate large tables of checks, understand overlapping requirements and dependencies, interpret different configuration states, and make decisions that could ultimately affect very large endpoint environments.

The interface often reflected how the underlying system worked rather than the decision the administrator was trying to make.

At enterprise scale, that complexity created both cognitive load and operational friction.

The solution

I redesigned the experience around three ideas: clearer decisions, progressive guidance and more explicit system feedback.

Rather than exposing every rule at once, the interface surfaces the information administrators need at the point where it becomes relevant.

The goal was not to remove the technical depth of a cybersecurity product. It was to make that complexity easier to understand and manage.

Outcome

66% reduction in checklist creation time From 35 minutes to under 12 minutes
~34% drop in support tickets Within the first quarter post-release
2 modules adopted the redesign pattern Patch and Software Distribution

Business context

BigFix is designed for organisations managing large fleets of endpoints.

Within Security Compliance Manager, administrators translate internal security policies and external cybersecurity benchmarks into configurations that can be deployed and monitored across endpoint groups.

A checklist is not simply a collection of independent items. Checks can overlap, depend on one another, be customised and evolve as the standards they originate from change.

The challenge was therefore bigger than simplifying one screen:

How could we make complex cybersecurity compliance behaviour understandable enough for administrators to make confident decisions at enterprise scale?

The broader SCM workflow

Security Compliance Manager was not a single-flow redesign. The modernisation covered a connected journey across checklist creation, deployment, maintenance, device targeting and compliance remediation.

At a product level, the administrator journey can be understood as:

Security policy / benchmark → Create and configure checklist → Choose target devices → Deploy → Monitor compliance → Remediate → Maintain and synchronise

This case study focuses on Create and Configure Checklist, because it captures many of the core design challenges present across the wider modernisation: dense information, dependencies, legacy constraints and expert-user workflows.

Understanding the user context

The primary user is a BigFix Administrator / Corporate IT Infrastructure and Operations administrator responsible for keeping endpoints compliant with company policies and external benchmarks.

They work closely with Corporate IT Security teams, who define the security policies. Their role is to translate those requirements into configurations that can be deployed, monitored and maintained across endpoint environments.

Their work is continuous rather than one-time. As organisational requirements and external standards evolve, administrators need to keep custom checklists aligned with those changes.

A typical workflow

01

Create a custom checklist

Build a checklist for a particular group of endpoints.

02

Add additional checks

Add checks when existing content does not fully cover the organisation's requirements.

03

Customise checks

Modify individual check content or parameter values to align with internal security policies.

04

Deploy to endpoints

Apply the checklist to the relevant target devices or groups.

05

Maintain and synchronise

Review updates from external checklists, compare differences, update the custom checklist and redeploy where necessary.

These administrators are domain experts, but their job is to manage cybersecurity policy and compliance—not to decode the internal architecture of the product.

The experience therefore needed to preserve technical depth while reducing unnecessary friction around relationships, dependencies and changes.

What was going wrong?

I first looked at where the existing checklist workflow required administrators to interpret the system themselves.

Several recurring problems emerged.

The interface used terminology that reflected the system rather than the administrator's task.

Actions did not always make their consequences obvious.

Feedback could describe what had happened without making the next step clear.

And when overlapping checks or dependencies appeared, their relationships could become difficult to understand.

Administrators were being asked to understand too much of the system model before they could make a decision.

Understanding the workflow

Before designing individual screens, I worked with product, engineering and domain experts to understand how the checklist workflow behaved behind the interface.

This mattered because a seemingly simple action could trigger several rules in the background.

Adding a check, for example, could reveal an existing relationship, introduce a dependency or require another decision before the checklist could be updated.

Mapping the workflow helped us separate what the system needed to know from what the administrator needed to know.

That distinction became the foundation for the redesign.

Exploring three approaches

Before moving into detailed UI, I explored three ways the experience could handle this complexity.

01

Show more information upfront

One approach was to expose more of the system logic before an administrator made a decision. This increased transparency, but it also increased cognitive load and presented information that was not always relevant to the immediate task.

02

Guide administrators progressively

The second approach revealed additional information when it became relevant. Administrators could begin with the immediate task, while dependencies or conflicts appeared in context as they progressed. This kept the main workflow simpler without hiding important information.

03

Automate more decisions

A third approach was to allow the system to resolve more situations automatically. This could reduce effort, but some decisions could not be safely automated and others were constrained by the existing architecture.

Choosing a direction

We moved forward with progressive guidance. It provided a balance between clarity, transparency and what the existing system could reliably support.

Principles that shaped the solution

Three principles guided the redesign.

Guide, don't expose

Show administrators the decision they need to make rather than forcing them to interpret internal system logic first.

Reveal complexity progressively

Not every rule needs to be visible at the same time. Additional information should appear when it becomes relevant.

Make consequences explicit

Actions, warnings and validation should clearly communicate what will happen next.

The key redesign

One of the biggest changes was restructuring the hierarchy of the experience.

Previously, system information, warnings and actions could compete for attention.

The redesign placed the administrator's current decision at the centre of the interface, with supporting information appearing around it when needed.

The redesign focused on four improvements:

Clearer hierarchy — The most important decision or action is easier to identify.

User-facing terminology — Language reflects what administrators are trying to accomplish rather than internal system behaviour.

Contextual guidance — Dependencies and related information appear when they matter.

More explicit feedback — The interface explains both what happened and what the administrator can do next.

Designing the core flow

The redesigned checklist workflow breaks a complex task into a sequence of smaller decisions.

01

Select

The administrator begins by selecting the checks they want to include.

02

Understand

If a selection introduces an overlap or dependency, the system explains the relationship in context.

03

Resolve

The administrator is given a clear action rather than being expected to interpret the system state themselves.

04

Review

Changes remain visible before confirmation so the administrator can understand what will be applied.

This reduced the amount of information that needed to be processed at any one moment while preserving the depth required for a cybersecurity compliance workflow.

Designing beyond the happy path

Much of the real complexity appeared outside the ideal flow.

The experience therefore also needed to remain understandable when administrators encountered duplicate checks, dependencies or validation issues.

Duplicate checks

The interface explains that the check already exists and clarifies what that means for the current action.

Dependencies

Related requirements are surfaced when they become relevant without exposing every relationship upfront.

Validation and warnings

Messages focus on the consequence of the action and the next decision the administrator needs to make.

Designing these states early helped ensure the experience remained coherent when real-world complexity appeared.

Designing within technical constraints

One direction we explored was simplifying check selection by allowing administrators to select checks based on criteria and security level, rather than working through a dense table.

This could have reduced cognitive load and made it faster to identify the right checks for a checklist.

The challenge was that checks came from different security guidelines, and those guidelines did not all follow the same conventions.

Supporting this approach properly would have required refactoring and standardising checks across those guidelines first.

BigFix could support that kind of change, but the amount of underlying restructuring required meant it was not feasible within the scope and timeline of this project.

Rather than designing an experience the existing system could not reliably support, I focused on improving the current selection model through clearer hierarchy, guidance and feedback.

The final experience

The final checklist experience shifted the workflow away from exposing system logic and toward helping administrators make a series of clear, informed decisions.

The result is an experience that preserves the depth required for enterprise cybersecurity while making that complexity easier to navigate.

What I took away

Complexity doesn't always need to be removed

In a cybersecurity product, some complexity is necessary. The design challenge is deciding when administrators need to see it, how much they need to understand and how the product can help them act on it.

Small design decisions compound

Terminology, hierarchy, feedback and interaction states can appear minor in isolation. Across a product used to manage cybersecurity compliance at the scale of hundreds of thousands of endpoints, those decisions collectively determine how understandable and dependable the experience feels.
